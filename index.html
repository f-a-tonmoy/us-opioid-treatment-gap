One Facility for Every Eighteen Deaths

Opioids killed more than 600,000 Americans in eleven years. Treatment followed the deaths. Five states got left behind.

In 2024, an American died of an opioid overdose roughly every 10 minutes. Two years earlier it was every 6. The improvement did not arrive evenly.

This analysis joins three federal datasets at the state level: every opioid overdose death certificate from 2014 through 20241, every substance-use treatment facility in SAMHSA’s national locator2, and Census population, income, and insurance figures3. It asks one question: do the states with the heaviest overdose burden have the treatment infrastructure to match?

Section 01The steepest drop on record.

In 2014 the country recorded 28,647 opioid deaths. By the 2022 peak it had nearly tripled, to 81,806, driven almost entirely by illegally made fentanyl. The worst year was 2020: the pandemic hit a street supply already flooded with fentanyl, and deaths jumped 38%. In 2024 the count fell to 54,045, down 32% in one year, the sharpest decline ever measured4. Nobody fully knows why. Wider naloxone access, a shifting drug supply, and fewer surviving long-term users all likely play a part, and the only earlier decline, a 2% dip in 2018, lasted one year. The progress is real. So is the limit: 2024’s toll still sits 8% above the last pre-pandemic year, and nearly double 2014’s.

Section 02The map the fentanyl era drew.

Averaged over 2022–2024 to smooth out the small states, West Virginia’s rate of 56 deaths per 100,000 residents is 2.6 times the national figure of 21, and 13 times Nebraska’s, the lowest. The highest rates run through Appalachia and the mid-Atlantic (West Virginia, Delaware, Tennessee, Kentucky), northern New England (Maine, Vermont), and, new this decade, up the Pacific coast. The Plains states have far lower rates.

Section 03Every state improved. Some barely.

Every state, and DC, now sits below its worst year; the size of the retreat varies widely. Arkansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, and Ohio have roughly halved their death rates. At the other end, Alaska is down just 5%, and in 2024 had the country’s highest unadjusted death rate (37.4 per 100k). Nevada and Washington have cut deaths at less than half the national pace; Arizona and Oregon are not far ahead. The epidemic is shifting west, and eleven years make it plain: Washington’s 2024 rate is 3.3 times its 2014 level, Alaska’s 3.6, against 1.8 nationally. Only New Hampshire and Utah sit below their 2014 rates, and both peaked early: New Hampshire in 2016, at the front of the fentanyl wave, Utah in 2014 itself.

Section 04Counting treatment capacity.

That is the burden half of the question. On the treatment side, SAMHSA’s locator lists 7,756 facilities providing medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD): buprenorphine or methadone. Of those, 2,037 are certified Opioid Treatment Programs (OTPs)5, the only tier allowed to dispense methadone. The spread is nearly nine-to-one: Maine, Kentucky, and West Virginia have five to six MOUD facilities per 100,000 residents. Texas has 0.72, the fewest anywhere, despite the fifth-largest death toll (2,438 in 2024) and the nation’s highest uninsured rate (16%).

What a facility count is not. A facility is not a bed, a clinician, or a patient slot. They show where treatment exists, not how much. Every finding below is checked against a second measure for that reason.

Section 05The Wyoming mirage.

Wyoming tops the capacity chart: 6.5 MOUD facilities per 100,000. It is an illusion built on small numbers. The state’s 38 listed facilities serve about 584,000 people scattered across nearly 98,000 square miles — and exactly one of them is a certified OTP. On methadone access, Wyoming ranks 50th of 51. One clinic opening or closing moves its headline figure nearly three percent.

The District of Columbia fails in the opposite direction. Its per-resident count looks unimpressive (2.1 per 100k), yet its 14 facilities sit within a few miles of every resident. Counting facilities per resident makes empty states look good and packed cities look bad. DC also has the second-highest combined death rate (46 per 100k), concentrated in a single city with no rural areas around it, so DC is flagged in every chart and kept out of the headline rankings. Rhode Island shows the same fragility in reverse: first in OTP density, 24th in MOUD.

No single measure here is trusted until a second one agrees. The states named next pass that test.

Section 06Mostly, treatment follows the burden.

The story most people would expect, that the hardest-hit states also have the least treatment, is wrong. Across all 50 states and DC, higher death rates come with more treatment per resident, not less (Spearman r = +0.42, p = 0.002). For the methadone-dispensing OTP tier the relationship is tighter still (r = +0.79, p < 0.0001): the OTP map closely follows the regions hit first, Appalachia and the Northeast. Two decades into the crisis, treatment has largely gone where the deaths were. Whether that is policy answering need, or simply time (the oldest epidemics have had the longest to build), this data cannot say.

Section 07Five states break the pattern.

Rank every state twice, by burden and by capacity, and the lines mostly run flat. The exceptions are the story. South Carolina, Washington, Tennessee, Nevada, and Alabama all rank 18 to 32 places worse on capacity than on burden, and all five sit in the top nine of 51 on the simplest measure: opioid deaths per treatment facility. Washington pairs the third-highest state death rate in 2024 with the 41st-ranked facility density.

Section 08The gap is not a poverty map.

The obvious explanation fails. States with too little treatment for their burden are not poorer (Spearman r = +0.01, p = 0.95) and not meaningfully less insured (r = +0.13, p = 0.35); the deaths-per-facility measure agrees (r = +0.11 and +0.22, both p > 0.1). Washington is rich and short on treatment; Alabama is poor and short on treatment. West Virginia is poor and well covered; Maryland is rich and well covered. Whatever decides where treatment lands (licensing rules, Medicaid history, the age of each state’s epidemic), it is not household income, and this dataset cannot see it.

What the numbers say

  1. Opioid deaths fell 32% in 2024 to 54,045, a record single-year fall. Seven of the eight weakest state declines are in the West; Alaska has barely joined the retreat.
  2. Treatment capacity broadly tracks burden (r = +0.42; +0.79 for methadone programs): the epidemic’s first-wave states built the most capacity.
  3. South Carolina, Washington, Tennessee, Nevada, and Alabama are the exceptions: high burden, little capacity, confirmed by two independent measures.
  4. Washington’s ratio of deaths to treatment facilities is the country’s worst; Texas has the fewest facilities per resident.
  5. Income and insurance coverage explain none of the gap; the states with too little treatment are not the poor ones.

LimitsWhat this data cannot tell us

Counts are not capacity. A facility that treats forty patients and one that treats four thousand count the same here. No public dataset reports patient slots per facility nationwide.

The locator is self-reported. Facilities describe their own services and update them on their own schedule. A clinic that quietly stopped prescribing still counts; a new one can take months to appear.

Office-based prescribing is invisible. Since Congress eliminated the buprenorphine waiver at the end of 2022, any DEA-registered clinician can prescribe it. Those prescribers, and telehealth, do not appear in a facility locator, so buprenorphine access is undercounted everywhere, most in states that lean on office-based care.

The timelines do not fully match. Facility data is a September 2026 snapshot; deaths run 2018–2024; population, income, and insurance are 2023 estimates. A state that opened clinics last year gets credit against deaths that came before them.

Age structure is left alone. The burden rankings use unadjusted rates. Re-ranking on age-adjusted rates moves no state more than 4 places (Maryland shifts most), so nothing above turns on it.

North Carolina’s 2023 undercount. NCHS reports ~900 overdose deaths coded late; North Carolina’s true burden rank is somewhat higher than shown.

DC is a city, not a state. Its extreme values on both burden and the gap are real but not comparable to states, and are flagged rather than ranked.

These numbers say where, not why. Fifty-one data points cannot say why treatment landed where it did, only where it visibly hasn’t.

Reproducibility

A multi-script Python (pandas/SciPy) pipeline generates everything above — fetch → clean/merge → analytics — with every number interpolated from the processed data at build time, none typed by hand.

  1. Mortality. CDC WONDER (no public sub-national API; queried via its web interface): database D157 for 2018–2024, D77 for 2014–2017, identical definitions (UCD X40–44, X60–64, X85, Y10–14; MCD T40.0–T40.4, T40.6; state × year). Merged on (FIPS, year), D157 preferred for the 2018–2020 overlap. All 153 overlapping state-year cells agree exactly; national totals match published NCHS figures to the death.
  2. Treatment capacity. One national FindTreatment.gov pull (exportsAsJson/v2): 12,220 listings − 549 same-name-and-address duplicates − 57 territory rows = 11,614 facilities in the 50 states + DC. OTP = SAMHSA certification flag (2,037, in line with the ~1,900+ SAMHSA reports); MOUD = buprenorphine or methadone used in treatment, or OTP (7,756). Naltrexone-only and “accepts MAT prescribed elsewhere” excluded.
  3. Population and socioeconomics. ACS 2023 1-year via the Census API: B01003 population (the denominator behind every per-100k facility rate), plus B19013 median household income and DP03 insurance coverage (the variables tested in Section 08). Labels verified against the API’s metadata; Puerto Rico dropped.
  4. Cleaning. WONDER’s trailing notes block stripped; numbers de-comma’d and coerced, with Suppressed/Unreliable markers mapped to missing (none occur at state level — checked, not assumed); FIPS zero-padded; state identity reconciled across all sources through one fixed 51-row name/abbreviation/FIPS table.
  5. Checks. Each source must match that table 51/51 or the pipeline halts; each WONDER file must land exactly 51 states × its years. Analysis is descriptive only (ranks, Spearman, n = 51; 2022–2024 combined rates to damp small-state noise), and no finding runs without a second measure agreeing.
