One Facility for Every Nineteen Deaths

Opioids killed more than 600,000 Americans in eleven years. Treatment followed the deaths. Four states got left behind.

In 2024, an American died of an opioid overdose roughly every 10 minutes. Two years earlier it was every 6. The improvement did not arrive evenly.

This analysis joins three federal datasets at the state level: every opioid overdose death certificate from 2014 through 20241, every substance-use treatment facility in SAMHSA’s national locator2, and Census population, income, and insurance figures3. It asks one question: do the states with the heaviest overdose burden have the treatment infrastructure to match?

Section 01The steepest drop on record.

In 2014 the country recorded 28,647 opioid deaths. By the 2022 peak it had nearly tripled, to 81,806, driven almost entirely by illegally made fentanyl. The worst year was 2020: the pandemic hit a street supply already flooded with fentanyl, and deaths jumped 38%. In 2024 the count fell to 54,045, down 32% in one year, the sharpest decline ever measured4. Nobody fully knows why. Wider naloxone access, a shifting drug supply, and fewer surviving long-term users all likely play a part, and the only earlier decline, a 2% dip in 2018, lasted one year. The progress is real. So is the limit: 2024’s toll still sits 8% above the last pre-pandemic year, and nearly double 2014’s.

Section 02The map the fentanyl era drew.

Averaged over 2022–2024 to smooth out the small states, West Virginia’s rate of 56 deaths per 100,000 residents is 2.5 times the national figure of 22, and 11 times Nebraska’s, the lowest. The highest rates run through Appalachia and the mid-Atlantic (West Virginia, Delaware, Tennessee, Kentucky), northern New England (Maine, Vermont), and, new this decade, up the Pacific coast. The Plains states have far lower rates.

Section 03What the death certificates leave out.

That map needs a correction before it can be trusted. A death certificate records an overdose, but naming the drug depends on whether the local coroner or medical examiner ordered a toxicology screen and wrote down what it found. Nationally, 4% of overdose deaths name no drug at all. That number is not spread evenly. In Louisiana it is 30%. In Connecticut it is under one percent.

States that investigate carefully therefore look worse than states that do not, purely as an artifact of record-keeping. The standard repair, used in the epidemiological literature6, is to assume the unnamed deaths in a state involved opioids at the same rate as that state’s deaths where a drug was named, and scale accordingly. Across 2022–2024 that lifts the national toll by 4%, and it can only be applied from 2018 on, because the older federal database was not queried the same way. Every headline count in this article is therefore the recorded one, which is what CDC publishes and what can be checked against it. The correction is used where it matters, comparing states against each other.

For most states the correction is cosmetic: 50 of the 51 move four places or fewer in the burden ranking. One state moves a lot. Louisiana rises 17 places, from 26th to 9th, which puts it in the worst ten in the country. Its reported rate of 23.3 per 100,000 becomes 33.2. Every ranking from here on uses the corrected figures, and the correction is the reason Louisiana appears in them at all.

Section 04Every state improved. Some barely.

Every state, and DC, now sits below its worst year; the size of the retreat varies widely. Arkansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, and Ohio have roughly halved their death rates. At the other end, Alaska is down just 5%, and in 2024 had the country’s highest unadjusted death rate (37.4 per 100k). Nevada and Washington have cut deaths at less than half the national pace; Arizona and Oregon are not far ahead. The epidemic is shifting west, and eleven years make it plain: Washington’s 2024 rate is 3.3 times its 2014 level, Alaska’s 3.6, against 1.8 nationally. Only New Hampshire and Utah sit below their 2014 rates, and both peaked early: New Hampshire in 2016, at the front of the fentanyl wave, Utah in 2014 itself.

Section 05Counting treatment capacity.

That is the burden half of the question. On the treatment side, SAMHSA’s locator lists 7,657 facilities providing medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD): buprenorphine or methadone. Of those, 2,100 are certified Opioid Treatment Programs (OTPs)5, the only tier allowed to dispense methadone. The spread is nearly nine-to-one: Maine, Kentucky, and West Virginia have four to six MOUD facilities per 100,000 residents. Texas has 0.71, the fewest anywhere, despite the fifth-largest death toll (2,438 in 2024) and the nation’s highest uninsured rate (16%).

Two measures, deliberately. The OTP count comes from SAMHSA’s official certification list. The MOUD count comes from what facilities say about themselves in the treatment locator. They are collected differently and fail differently, which is exactly why every finding below has to satisfy both. A facility is also not a bed, a clinician, or a patient slot: these counts show where treatment exists, not how much of it there is.

Section 06The Wyoming mirage.

Wyoming tops the capacity chart: 6.5 MOUD facilities per 100,000, the best rate in the country. It is an illusion built on small numbers. The state’s 38 listed facilities serve about 584,000 people scattered across nearly 98,000 square miles, and not one of them is a certified OTP. Wyoming is the only state in the country where a person cannot get methadone from a licensed program without leaving it.

Finding that took two attempts. The treatment locator asks facilities to declare their own credentials, and on that self-reported flag Wyoming appeared to have one certified program. SAMHSA’s official certification list5 says zero. Nationally the two sources look interchangeable, 2,037 against 2,100, which is how the error survived a first check. State by state they are not, so every OTP count here comes from the official list.

West Virginia’s 9 is itself not an accident. It is the only state with a standing moratorium on new methadone clinics, layered with zoning rules that keep them half a mile from any school or daycare9. The state with the worst overdose burden in the country has written a cap on the most regulated form of treatment into its own law.

Distance is the part none of these counts capture. Among Medicare patients, a drive of 15 minutes instead of 5 was associated with roughly half the likelihood of receiving methadone at all10. A state can look adequately supplied and still be unreachable.

Section 07Mostly, treatment follows the burden.

The story most people would expect, that the hardest-hit states also have the least treatment, is wrong. Across all 50 states and DC, higher death rates come with more treatment per resident, not less (Spearman r = +0.40, p = 0.004). For the methadone-dispensing OTP tier the relationship is tighter still (r = +0.67, p < 0.0001): the OTP map closely follows the regions hit first, Appalachia and the Northeast. A decade into the fentanyl era, treatment has largely gone where the deaths were.

What that relationship cannot settle is which way it runs. Capacity may have followed need, or need may have been slower to fall where treatment was scarce, or both may reflect how old each state’s epidemic is. The treatment counts are a 2026 snapshot and the deaths run 2022 through 2024, so the ordering in time cannot separate those.

The comparison is also worth holding at arm’s length. Nationally, only about a quarter of people who need treatment for opioid use disorder receive any medication for it8. What follows ranks states against each other, not against a standard any of them meets.

Section 08Four states break the pattern.

Rank every state twice, by burden and by capacity, and the lines mostly run flat. The exceptions are the story, and picking them out calls for a rule rather than an eye. A state qualifies here if its corrected death rate is above the national median and it lands in the worst quarter of states on three separate measures of shortfall: the gap between its two ranks, how far it sits below the capacity its burden predicts, and the blunt count of opioid deaths per treatment facility. Those measures are built differently but agree closely (r = +0.83). DC is charted throughout but left out of the ranking, being a single city measured against states.

South Carolina, Washington, Tennessee, and Nevada clear all three. They rank 18 to 31 places worse on capacity than on burden. Washington pairs the third-highest state death rate in 2024 with the 41st-ranked facility density.

Washington and Nevada combine thin coverage with the country’s weakest 2024 declines. Alaska, kept off the list because 277 deaths a year make its rates jumpy, looks much the same: seventh in corrected burden, 12 deaths per facility. Where deaths are falling slowest, treatment is hardest to find.

Two states fail the rule in a way worth naming. Alabama and Texas have the thinnest coverage in the country, 1.04 and 0.71 facilities per 100,000, and Alabama carries 16 deaths for each one. Both are excluded only because their death rates sit below the national median. That is not reassurance so much as a description of exposure: the capacity is already missing, and nothing about the last decade suggests burden stays put.

Section 09The recovery is not shared.

The geography of the gap is only one dimension of it. Split the same deaths by race and the national decline stops looking like one story.

White Americans’ opioid death rate peaked in 2021 and by 2024 had returned to +1% of its 2018 level, essentially back to where the fentanyl era started. No other group is close. Black Americans’ rate peaked two years later, in 2023, and remains 64% above 2018. For American Indian and Alaska Native Americans it is 133% above. In 2023 the Black rate (37.5 per 100,000) was more than half again the White rate (23.3), a reversal of how this epidemic was described for most of its first decade. The Black share of opioid deaths rose from 13% in 2018 to 22% in 2023.

Treatment access follows the same fault line. Studied across commercially insured patients, Black patients had roughly 40% lower odds of receiving either buprenorphine or methadone than white patients7, and the two medications are not distributed alike: methadone, which requires supervised daily dosing at a clinic, is concentrated in urban Black and Hispanic communities, while buprenorphine, which can be prescribed in an office, reaches whiter and wealthier ones. The four-state gap in this article is measured in facilities per resident. This one is not visible in facility counts at all.

Section 10What money explains, and what policy does.

The obvious explanation fails. States with too little treatment for their burden are not poorer (Spearman r = -0.07, p = 0.64) and not meaningfully less insured (r = +0.17, p = 0.22); the deaths-per-facility measure agrees (r = +0.11 and +0.23, both p > 0.09). Washington is rich and short on treatment; Alabama is poor and short on treatment. West Virginia is poor and well covered; Maryland is rich and well covered. Household income is not the variable.

One policy variable does separate them. The ten states that never adopted the Medicaid expansion11 have about half the treatment density of the states that did: a median of 1.46 MOUD facilities per 100,000 against 2.72 (p = 0.007), and 0.37 certified OTPs against 0.68 (p = 0.014). Their overdose burden, meanwhile, is statistically indistinguishable (p = 0.22). Same problem, half the infrastructure.

This explains part of the gap, not all of it. South Carolina and Tennessee never expanded; Nevada and Washington did, and are short of treatment anyway. And the comparison is between fifty-one jurisdictions that differ in a hundred other ways at the same time, so it identifies an association, not a mechanism. What can be said is that the thing income failed to explain, a single coverage decision partly does.

What the numbers say

  1. Opioid deaths fell 32% in 2024 to 54,045, a record single-year fall. Seven of the eight weakest state declines are in the West; Alaska has barely joined the retreat.
  2. Treatment capacity broadly tracks burden (r = +0.40; +0.67 for methadone programs), though which way that relationship runs cannot be settled here.
  3. South Carolina, Washington, Tennessee, and Nevada are the exceptions, chosen by a rule that requires above-median burden and worst-quartile shortfall on three separate measures.
  4. White Americans’ death rate is back to its 2018 level. Black Americans’ is 64% higher, and American Indian and Alaska Native Americans’ is 133% higher.
  5. Income and insurance explain none of the gap. Medicaid expansion explains part: non-expansion states carry the same burden with about half the treatment.

LimitsWhat this data cannot tell us

Counts are not capacity, and distance is not measured. A facility that treats forty patients and one that treats four thousand count the same here, and the field’s usual access measure is travel time rather than facilities per resident. SAMHSA’s facility survey does collect client counts, and drive times need road networks and sub-state population; this analysis uses neither, so it shows where treatment exists rather than how much there is or who can reach it.

Opioids are only part of the burden. Most opioid deaths now also involve stimulants, and medication for opioid use disorder does nothing for methamphetamine or cocaine. Capacity measured this way answers a narrower question than the death counts pose.

The locator is self-reported. Facilities describe their own services and update them on their own schedule. A clinic that quietly stopped prescribing still counts; a new one can take months to appear.

Office-based prescribing is invisible. Since Congress eliminated the buprenorphine waiver at the end of 2022, any DEA-registered clinician can prescribe it. Those prescribers, and telehealth, do not appear in a facility locator, so buprenorphine access is undercounted everywhere, most in states that lean on office-based care.

The timelines do not fully match. Facility data is a September 2026 snapshot; deaths run 2014–2024; population, income, and insurance are 2023 estimates, so a state that opened clinics last year gets credit against deaths that came before them. Deaths stop at 2024 because that is where final data ends: CDC publishes provisional counts closer to the present, but they are revised for months and are incomplete in ways that vary by state, which would put reporting speed into the rankings.

States are not independent observations. Drug supply, policy, and patients all cross state lines, and people travel out of state for treatment, especially for methadone. The correlations here treat fifty-one jurisdictions as separate points when neighboring states are not.

The correction is a simplification. Redistributing unspecified deaths in proportion to a state’s named ones assumes the missing certificates resemble the recorded ones. The published method models local characteristics as well; this one does not, and Louisiana’s adjustment in particular rests on that assumption.

Exact ranks carry more precision than the data does. Alaska’s 2024 rate of 37.4 per 100,000 has a confidence interval running 33 to 42, and 2 states’ intervals overlap West Virginia’s. Re-ranking on age-adjusted rather than unadjusted rates also moves states by up to 4 places (Maryland most). Neither changes anything above, because the findings rest on groups of states rather than exact positions.

Reproducibility

A multi-script Python (pandas/SciPy) pipeline generates everything above — fetch → clean/merge → analytics — with every number interpolated from the processed data at build time, none typed by hand.

  1. Mortality. CDC WONDER (no public sub-national API; queried via its web interface): database D157 for 2018–2024, D77 for 2014–2017, identical definitions (UCD X40–44, X60–64, X85, Y10–14; MCD T40.0–T40.4, T40.6; state × year). Merged on (FIPS, year), D157 preferred for the 2018–2020 overlap. All 153 overlapping state-year cells agree exactly; national totals match published NCHS figures to the death.
  2. Drug-specificity correction. Two further WONDER queries on the same UCD codes, state × year, 2018–2024: one unrestricted (every drug overdose death), one with MCD restricted to T36–T50.8 (deaths naming at least one specific drug). Adjusted opioid deaths = reported × total ÷ specified, applied per state-year. National lift 4%; 50 of 51 states move four ranks or fewer.
  3. Treatment capacity. Two sources, kept separate on purpose. Certified OTPs come from SAMHSA’s official directory (2,100 in the 50 states + DC). MOUD facilities come from one national FindTreatment.gov pull (exportsAsJson/v2): 12,220 listings − 549 same-name-and-address duplicates − 57 territory rows = 11,614 facilities, of which 7,657 report using buprenorphine or methadone. An earlier version derived OTP counts from the locator’s self-reported certification flag; that flag matches the directory nationally (2,037 vs 2,100) but not by state, and was discarded.
  4. Population and socioeconomics. ACS 2023 1-year via the Census API: B01003 population (the denominator behind every per-100k facility rate), plus B19013 median household income and DP03 insurance coverage (the variables tested in Section 10). Labels verified against the API’s metadata; Puerto Rico dropped. Medicaid expansion status is KFF’s classification, held as a fixed 10-state list.
  5. Cleaning. WONDER’s trailing notes block stripped; numbers de-comma’d and coerced, with Suppressed/Unreliable markers mapped to missing (none occur at state level — checked, not assumed); FIPS zero-padded; state identity reconciled across all sources through one fixed 51-row name/abbreviation/FIPS table.
  6. Checks. Each source must match that table 51/51 or the pipeline halts; each WONDER file must land exactly 51 states × its years. Analysis is descriptive only (ranks, Spearman, Mann–Whitney, n = 51; 2022–2024 combined rates to damp small-state noise). The underserved states are selected by rule, not by hand, and the reader-facing rank gap is validated against regression residuals (r = +0.83). No finding runs without a second measure agreeing.
